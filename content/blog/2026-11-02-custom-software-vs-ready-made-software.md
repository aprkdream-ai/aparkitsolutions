Most businesses should not build everything themselves. Most also reach a point where a ready-made product stops fitting. The useful question is not which option is better in general, but which one suits this particular process in your business.

## What ready-made software does well

Ready-made software, whether a SaaS subscription or a packaged product, gives you something working almost immediately. Someone else handles updates, hosting, and security patches, and the product already reflects what thousands of other customers needed.

It is usually the right choice when:

- The process is common, such as payroll, email, or standard bookkeeping
- Doing it the standard way gives you no disadvantage
- You need something working this week
- A small team has no one to own a custom system

If a good product covers your needs, use it. Building your own accounting package rarely makes sense when the rules are the same for everyone.

## Where ready-made software starts to hurt

The warning signs tend to appear gradually:

- Staff keep a spreadsheet beside the system because it cannot do one important thing
- Information is typed into two or three tools because they do not talk to each other
- You change how you work to match the software, and customers notice
- Per-user pricing grows faster than the value you get
- A feature you depend on sits on someone else's roadmap, with no date

One of these is often tolerable. Several together usually mean the workarounds now cost more than they appear to.

## What custom software does well

Custom software is built around how your business actually runs. It makes the most sense when the process is part of what sets you apart, such as how you quote, schedule, serve customers, or manage compliance.

You also control the roadmap and the data, and you own the code. New features and integrations are added when the business needs them.

The trade-offs are real. It costs more up front, takes longer to arrive, and needs maintenance for as long as you use it. A partner who plays those down is not giving you the full picture.

## Compare the true cost

A monthly subscription and a development quote are hard to compare directly. Look at the same period, such as three to five years, on both sides.

| Cost to include | Ready-made | Custom |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Licences or subscriptions | Ongoing, often per user | Usually none for the software itself |
| Build or setup | Lower | Higher |
| Maintenance and hosting | Mostly included | Your responsibility, often through a support plan |
| Workarounds and double entry | Can be significant, and easy to miss | Lower when designed out |
| Changing later | Depends on data export options | You own the code and data |

Put a rough figure on staff time spent on workarounds. It is usually the largest number that nobody has written down.

## The middle path

This is rarely all or nothing. Many businesses keep ready-made tools for standard work and build custom software only for the part that makes them different, then connect everything through [APIs](/services/api-development-integration).

A common pattern is to keep your accounting package, and build the operations system around it. You could also start with a focused tool for the most painful process, prove its value, and grow from there.

## Questions to ask before deciding

1. Is this process a real part of how we compete, or is it a routine task?
2. How many workarounds do we rely on today?
3. What happens to our data if we leave this vendor?
4. Who will own the system after launch?
5. What is the smallest version that would solve the main problem?
